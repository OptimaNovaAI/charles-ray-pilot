01

Start your day

Two minutes in the morning, and the day is pointed at what matters.
The morning command center
Everything that needs you, gathered and ranked, before the coffee is poured.
"Check my inbox and my calendar. Tell me what's due today, who's waiting on me, and what I should tackle first."
The two-minute triage
The important few, surfaced first. No scrolling, no guessing.
"Summarize what came into my inbox since yesterday and tell me what needs my attention most urgently."
Close the day clean
Nothing carries over silently. Tomorrow's list starts building tonight.
"Before I wrap up: what came in today that I haven't answered, and what should be first on tomorrow morning's list?"
02

Work a case

Frame the matter, pressure-test the strategy, and see the other side's best punch first.
Decode any IRS notice
Dense IRS language, stripped down to what actually matters.
"I received IRS letter [number]. Explain in plain English what it means, what they're asking for, my deadline, and my options."
Turn a notice into a to-do list
The thinking is done. You just work the list.
"Read the IRS notice in my inbox from [client name] and turn it into a checklist of what I need to do and by when."
Make Claude play the IRS examiner
See what they would challenge before they challenge it.
"You are an IRS examiner reviewing my client's position. What would you challenge, and what would you ask for to support or refute it? Be tough."
Let it argue against you
A sparring partner that catches the soft spots before opposing counsel does.
"Here's the direction I'm leaning on this matter. Poke holes in it. Where is it weak, and what facts or law would make it stronger?"
Weigh two options side by side
A clear-eyed comparison before you commit to a direction.
"Here are two ways I could respond to the IRS on this. Lay out the pros, the cons, and the risks of each."
When a levy is looming
The house-on-fire case. Get oriented in minutes, not hours.
"The IRS is preparing to levy my client's accounts. Walk me through the options to stop or delay it, including a collection due process hearing: what each requires, the deadlines, and what I should confirm first."
Run the statute clock
The ten-year collection statute is your weapon. Make sure the math is on your side.
"Walk me through the collection statute math on this account: when the ten-year clock started, what could have paused it, and when it likely expires. Flag every date I need to verify."
Issue-spot a document
Scope the problem before you commit hours to it.
"Review this document. Identify the nature and scope of the problem, issue-spot the areas of tax law involved, and tell me what additional facts I should gather."
The two-document split (your move)
The prompt you invented in week two. Structure the advocacy, separate the facts.
"Separate this into two documents: a statement of legal authority and position, and a separate statement of facts made by the taxpayer."
03

Write in your voice

Claude is connected to your email and trained on how you write. Put it to work.
Reply to a real email, in your voice
It reads the thread, drafts the reply, and leaves it in your drafts. You review, tweak a word, send.
"Read the latest email from [client name] and draft my reply in my voice. Put it in my drafts so I can review before it goes out."
Turn call notes into a client update
You do the thinking on the call. Claude does the assembling afterward.
"Turn these notes into a clear, warm update to my client in my voice, explaining where things stand and what's next."
Confirm what was agreed
The follow-up that protects everyone: what was said, what happens next, in writing.
"Here are my notes from today's call with [client name]. Draft a follow-up email in my voice confirming what we discussed and what happens next."
Firm, but still kind
Holds the line and the warmth at the same time. You stay the good guy and still move the case.
"Draft a firm but respectful email to a client who hasn't returned their documents. Keep it warm, but make the urgency clear."
What happens after they sign
The question every new client asks, answered warmly and consistently.
"A client asked what happens after they sign the POA. Draft a short reply in my voice walking them through the next steps."
Draft a whole batch at once
One instruction, a stack of finished drafts waiting for your review.
"Find every client who hasn't returned a signed power of attorney and draft a gentle reminder to each one, in my voice, into my drafts."
04

Find anything

Your inbox, searched for you. Five minutes of scrolling becomes one sentence.
Find any email in seconds
It brings back the exact thread, and what was said.
"Find the email where [client name] sent me their [document], and pull up what they said."
Find out who you're waiting on
Reads your sent mail and flags the open loops.
"Look through my sent mail and tell me which clients I'm waiting to hear back from, so I know who to follow up with."
Catch what's gone quiet
A weekly sweep so nothing gets truly lost.
"Find anything in my inbox that has been sitting more than a week without a response from me."
A one-page brief before any call
Walk in already up to speed, prepared in a minute instead of re-reading a month of back-and-forth.
"Pull everything from my email about [client name] and give me a one-page brief on where things stand before my call."
Prep for the practitioner line
Everything on the matter in one place before you dial the 8 AM line.
"Before I call the IRS practitioner line about [client name], pull everything from my email on their matter: tax years at issue, what's been filed, balances mentioned, and open questions."
05

Work smarter with Claude

The habits that turn a tool into a thinking partner.
The RCTC frame
Raw Cookies Taste Crummy: Role, Context, Task, Constraints. When a prompt underdelivers, it's usually missing one of these four.
"You are [role]. Context: [the situation]. Task: [what I need]. Constraints: [format, length, tone]."
Let Claude interview you
Instead of guessing what to include, it pulls the details out of you, one at a time.
"Ask me questions one at a time until you have everything you need to draft this, then write it."
You never have to start over
Small nudges until it's exactly right. Refining, not restarting.
"Make it shorter." / "Make it warmer." / "Give me two versions." / "More formal, this one's for the IRS."
Tell it to think first
On anything with layers, you'll feel the difference in what comes back.
"Before you answer, think through this carefully, step by step."
The one habit that ties it all together
It gathers the facts first, the way a sharp associate would. This is the whole game.
"Before you answer, ask me anything you need to give me your best work."
See everything it can do
Ask once, get the whole map of what's now possible, tailored to your practice.
"Now that you're connected to my email, be as comprehensive as possible about everything you can do for me. Walk me through how you can support my practice, from the moment a client email arrives to the moment a matter closes. Don't hold back."
06

Now that Claude lives in your Outlook

These run in the Claude panel inside Outlook, where it can read the email in front of you and place drafts straight into your Drafts folder. Live since July 21.
Reply to whatever is open
The core move. Open an email in Outlook, tap Claude, and it already knows what it is looking at.
"Read this email and draft my reply. Keep it short and direct, and tell me anything I should verify before I send."
Get the gist of a long thread
Twelve messages deep and you just got added? Start here.
"Summarize this thread in five lines, then tell me exactly what it is asking of me."
Stop typing, start talking
Tap the microphone in the panel and dictate it out loud. Your voice is faster than your hands, every time.
"Draft a reply telling them I can take the matter, and ask for the notice and the last two years of returns."
The seven-day sweep
Your Monday morning, handled in one prompt.
"Show me everything from the past seven days that still needs a response from me, ranked by urgency, then draft the top three."
Turn your inbox into a to-do list
Not a summary. A list of what you owe people, with names and dates.
"Check my email for the last five days and give me a to-do list of what I owe people, with names and dates."
Who is waiting on me right now
The follow-ups that fall through the cracks are the ones that cost cases.
"Look through my inbox and my sent mail. Tell me who is waiting on a response from me and how long they have been waiting."
Find it, then answer it
Search and draft in a single instruction.
"Find the email where [client name] sent me [document]. Pull up what they said, then draft my reply."
Draft the whole batch
This is where the time really comes back. One prompt, a dozen drafts, all waiting in your Outlook Drafts folder.
"Find every client who has not returned a signed power of attorney and draft a gentle reminder to each one."
Standing: your signature on every draft
The Outlook panel forgets between sessions. Paste your signature instruction first thing, once per session, and every draft signs itself. The full version is in the email titled "Your signature on every Claude draft."
"Every email you draft for me must end with my official firm signature block, reproduced exactly as I gave it to you. Verbatim, once, at the very end. Confirm you have it."
Standing: default to seven days
Paste once at claude.ai under Settings, then Personalization, and it holds everywhere.
"When I ask for my morning triage or an inbox review, default to the past 7 days unless I say otherwise. Surface anything needing a response, anything from a government agency or opposing counsel, and any lead that has not yet been answered."
